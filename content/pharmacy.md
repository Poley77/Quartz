# Pharmacy

**Summary**: Prescription drug spending is one of the fastest-growing cost drivers in U.S. healthcare, shaped by the rise of specialty biologics, GLP-1 therapies, and sweeping changes to how drugs are priced, distributed, and reimbursed.

**Sources**: `What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond.md`, `Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs.md`, `The U.S. Health Care System in 2026_ Inside the Great Healthcare Plan.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-12

---

## Scale and Growth

Drug net spending rose **11% from 2023 to 2024** — faster than any other category of healthcare spending. Pharmacy costs grew at **9% annually** between 2021 and 2024, compared to 7% for medical costs overall. (Source: McKinsey)

U.S. gross drug spending is projected to reach **$990 billion by 2029**, growing roughly 8% annually. The pharmacy services segment is expected to reach **$114 billion in EBITDA by 2029**, growing at 6% annually.

The U.S. spends roughly **twice as much per capita on prescription drugs** as peer nations — a gap that has persisted for decades despite repeated policy efforts. (Source: `Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs.md`)

## The GLP-1 Effect

GLP-1 agonists (e.g., Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro) accounted for roughly **half of the 11% increase in drug spending in 2024**. These drugs treat obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and adoption is still accelerating.

Employers are struggling with coverage decisions. Many dropped coverage for weight-loss indications in 2026 due to cost burden, while retaining coverage for diabetes. Factors that may moderate costs over time:

- A growing **direct-to-consumer market** with lower prices
- New **oral GLP-1 pills** that are easier to administer and potentially cheaper than injectables
- Virtual care solutions to manage utilization and outcomes

GLP-1 adoption is also putting pressure on [[employer-based-insurance|employer-sponsored plans]] and [[medicare|Medicare Advantage]], where it has contributed to margin compression. See [[healthcare-costs]] for the broader cost picture.

## Specialty Drugs and Biologics

Biologics overtook small-molecule drugs as a share of total spending between 2019 and 2024 — rising from 43% to 54% of net drug expenditure. This shift is driven by specialty therapies including GLP-1s, cancer drugs, immunologics, and emerging cell and gene therapies.

High-cost specialty drugs are frequently administered in hospital outpatient departments (HOPDs), which contributes to hospitals' disproportionate share of overall spending growth. See [[hospitals]] for more on HOPD economics.

**Fastest-growing pharmacy sub-segments (2024–2029 projected):**
- Hospital specialty pharmacy: ~21% annually
- Ambulatory infusion: ~9% annually

## The Pharmacy Value Chain

Prescription drugs pass through multiple intermediaries before reaching patients:

- **Manufacturers** — develop, produce, and set list prices; largest projected EBITDA segment ($368B by 2029)
- **Wholesalers/distributors** — buy from manufacturers and sell to pharmacies; projected $13B EBITDA
- **Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs)** — negotiate rebates from manufacturers, build formularies, and process claims for insurers and employers; projected $18B EBITDA
- **Retail and mail pharmacies** — dispense drugs to patients; projected $19B EBITDA
- **Specialty pharmacy and infusion services** — handle high-cost, complex biologics; projected $66B EBITDA — the fastest-growing segment

## Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs)

PBMs sit between manufacturers and payers, negotiating rebates in exchange for formulary placement. Critics argue the rebate system inflates list prices, reduces competition, and doesn't consistently lower costs for patients.

**Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026** introduced significant PBM reforms:
- Moved PBM compensation to a **flat service-based fee** (instead of a percentage of drug cost) for Medicare
- Required drug rebates to be **fully passed through** to plan sponsors for Medicare and group health plans
- Added new reporting and disclosure requirements

Congress and the Trump administration have both signaled interest in further PBM reform. The FTC has also increased scrutiny of PBM practices and drug pricing.

## Federal Drug Pricing Policy

**Inflation Reduction Act (IRA)**: Authorized Medicare to negotiate prices directly with manufacturers on a defined list of high-cost drugs. Some beneficiaries and the federal government saw lower costs, but the U.S. still pays more than peer nations for the same drugs.

**Most Favored Nation Pricing**: The Trump administration launched **TrumpRx.gov** to negotiate individual cash-price deals directly with pharmaceutical companies. Because most Americans use insurance rather than cash, the practical impact may be limited.

**Tariffs**: The threat of Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs created uncertainty in 2026. Some insurers raised 2026 premiums in anticipation of higher drug import costs.

## 340B Drug Pricing Program

The 340B program requires manufacturers to sell outpatient drugs at steep discounts to qualifying hospitals and health centers. Discounts through 340B grew **11% annually between 2020 and 2024**, intensifying competition for prescriptions between providers, payers, and PBMs. The program is a significant EBITDA driver for hospital systems — and a source of ongoing policy controversy.

## Distribution Models in Transition

Traditional pharmacy distribution through retail and mail-order pharmacies is being challenged by:

- **Cost-plus pricing models** — pharmacies align to a common pricing index, improving transparency and reducing variation
- **Direct-to-consumer (DTC) models** — pharmaceutical companies increasingly sell directly to patients, bypassing PBMs and traditional channels
- **Ambulatory and home infusion** — patients receiving complex biologics at home or in outpatient settings rather than hospital outpatient departments

These shifts are intensifying competition between vertically integrated pharmacy chains (e.g., CVS/Caremark, Express Scripts/Cigna) and independent pharmacies.

## Medicare Part D

[[medicare|Medicare Part D]] covers outpatient prescription drugs for Medicare beneficiaries. The IRA made structural changes to Part D including adding an out-of-pocket cap and shifting more cost-sharing to manufacturers. Part D drug expenses contributed to rising costs for [[medicare|Medicare Advantage]] plans in 2024.
