# Medicare Advantage: A Policy Primer

**Summary**: A structured overview of how Medicare Advantage works — its structure, how plans are paid, enrollment trends, quality measurement, and the key policy debates shaping its future.

## What Medicare Advantage Is

Medicare Advantage (MA), also called Medicare Part C, is the private plan alternative to traditional fee-for-service [[medicare|Medicare]]. Instead of receiving care through the government-administered program, MA enrollees choose a private health insurance plan that contracts with CMS to provide all Medicare-covered benefits — and often supplemental benefits not covered by traditional Medicare (dental, vision, hearing, fitness programs, transportation).

In exchange, CMS pays the plan a capitated rate per enrollee per month rather than paying providers directly for each service.

## Enrollment Growth

MA has grown from a minor program to the dominant form of Medicare coverage:

- 2005: ~13% of Medicare beneficiaries in MA
- 2015: ~31%
- 2024: ~54% — more than half of all Medicare beneficiaries

This growth has been driven by supplemental benefits, lower cost-sharing in many plans, and care coordination that traditional Medicare doesn't provide. Enrollment is projected to continue growing as the Baby Boom generation ages into Medicare.

## How Plans Are Paid

CMS sets a **county-level benchmark** — an estimate of what traditional Medicare would spend per beneficiary in that area. Plans submit a **bid** for what they expect their costs to be. If the bid is below the benchmark, the plan and CMS split the difference: the plan retains part of the savings as a rebate that must be used to fund supplemental benefits or reduced cost-sharing.

Payments are adjusted for each enrollee's health status using [[risk-adjustment-coding|HCC risk scores]] — sicker enrollees generate higher payments. See [[how-risk-adjustment-affects-payment-medicare-advantage-plans]] for detail on how this works and why it's contested.

## Quality Measurement: Star Ratings

CMS rates MA plans on a 1–5 star scale across roughly 40 measures in several domains:

- **Staying healthy**: Preventive screenings, vaccinations, BMI monitoring
- **Managing chronic conditions**: Diabetes care, blood pressure control, medication adherence
- **Member experience**: CAHPS survey results on access, care quality, and plan responsiveness
- **Patient outcomes**: Hospital readmissions, medication adherence rates

Plans with 4 or 5 stars receive **quality bonus payments** from CMS — additional funding on top of base capitation. These bonuses are substantial (5% or more of base payment) and create strong incentives for plans to invest in quality improvement and member engagement. Plans with low star ratings face payment reductions and potential sanctions.

## Quality Reporting and the Bonus Program

### How the Quality Bonus Program Works

Plans that achieve 4 or more stars receive a **quality bonus payment (QBP)** — an increase to their county-level benchmark, which in turn increases the rebate available to fund supplemental benefits and lower cost-sharing. The bonus is effectively a multiplier on the base payment: a 5% increase at 4 stars, more at 4.5 and 5 stars.

The financial stakes are significant. Quality bonuses across the MA program totalled **$11.6 billion in 2021** and are projected to reach at least **$12.7 billion in 2025** — more than four times the 2015 level. For an individual plan, crossing the 4-star threshold can mean tens of millions of dollars in additional annual revenue, as the diagram above illustrates.

### The Star Ratings Inflation Problem

The bonus program was designed to reward high performers. In practice, it has become nearly universal. Key trends:

- In 2015, only about 38% of MA enrollees were in 4+ star plans
- By 2022, that figure had risen to **90%** — almost the entire market was receiving quality bonuses
- After CMS recalibrated its methodology, the share fell to ~72% in 2023, but remains far above the program's original intent
- The average star rating across all contracts in 2023 was **4.15 stars** — meaning the average plan is a bonus-qualifying plan

When the vast majority of plans receive bonuses, the program no longer differentiates on quality — it becomes a near-universal payment add-on rather than a genuine incentive mechanism.

### Measurement Problems

The star rating system has several structural weaknesses that undermine its validity as a quality signal:

**Process over outcomes**: Many star rating measures focus on whether a care process occurred — was a cancer screening ordered? was a diabetes test administered? — rather than whether the patient's health actually improved. This means plans can score well by completing administrative steps without improving clinical outcomes.

**Plan-reported data**: A significant portion of star rating data is submitted by the plans themselves, creating an inherent conflict of interest. Research has found that some MA plans underreported hospital readmissions by failing to record the initial admission, inflating their apparent performance on readmission measures.

**Contract-level aggregation**: Star ratings are assigned at the contract level, not the plan level. A single contract can cover plans operating across different states and serving very different populations — including specialized plans for people with serious chronic illnesses or disabilities alongside general plans. Averaging performance across these populations obscures meaningful variation.

**No social risk adjustment**: The ratings do not adequately adjust for the social risk factors of a plan's enrolled population. Plans serving lower-income, higher-need populations face a structural disadvantage in star ratings — not because they deliver worse care, but because their members face more barriers to care that affect measurable outcomes. This creates a perverse incentive to avoid enrolling disadvantaged populations.

**Gaming the threshold**: Research has found that contracts whose ratings fluctuated near the 4-star bonus threshold were more likely to receive quality bonuses despite only modest differences in average performance — suggesting that plans focus resources on crossing the threshold rather than improving broadly.

### Does the Bonus Program Improve Quality?

The evidence is discouraging. Multiple independent analyses — including studies published in peer-reviewed journals and analyses by MedPAC, the GAO, and the Urban Institute — have found **no consistent relationship between quality bonus payments and actual quality improvement** in MA.

The pattern: star ratings have risen steadily over time, but this appears to reflect measurement gaming, methodological changes, and threshold management rather than genuine care improvements. Spending on quality bonuses has increased dramatically; evidence of commensurate patient benefit has not.

The GAO and MedPAC have both called for fundamental reform of the QBP, noting that the current system:
- Distributes bonuses too broadly to create meaningful incentives
- Uses measures that don't reliably track patient outcomes
- Generates billions in extra payments with limited accountability for results

### Reform Directions

Proposed and discussed reforms to the quality bonus program include:

- **Raising the threshold**: Requiring 4.5 or 5 stars for full bonus eligibility, to restore differentiation
- **Outcome-based measures**: Shifting the measure set away from process measures toward patient health outcomes, with independent data sources rather than plan-reported data
- **Social risk adjustment**: Adjusting benchmarks or measures to account for the socioeconomic profile of enrolled populations, so plans serving disadvantaged groups aren't penalized
- **Absolute vs. relative standards**: Tying bonuses to improvement over time rather than static performance levels, so plans can be rewarded for getting better even if they don't reach a high absolute threshold
- **Contract restructuring**: Disaggregating ratings at the plan level rather than the contract level to provide more accurate signals

## Key Policy Debates

**Overpayment**: Evidence suggests MA plans are paid more than traditional Medicare would spend on the same beneficiaries, due to coding intensity and favorable selection. CMS has been gradually adjusting payment methodology to address this. See [[taking-stock-medicare-advantage-payment]].

**Prior authorization**: MA plans use prior authorization more extensively than traditional Medicare. Critics argue this delays or denies necessary care; plans argue it reduces waste. CMS finalized new rules in 2024 tightening prior authorization requirements.

**Network adequacy**: MA plans use provider networks that may limit access compared to traditional Medicare's open-access model. CMS has strengthened network adequacy standards, but adequacy in rural areas remains a concern.

**Supplemental benefits**: The supplemental benefits that drive MA enrollment — dental, vision, hearing — are funded partly through the rebate system. As CMS tightens payment rates, plans face pressure to reduce these benefits, creating political risk.

## The MA-VBC Connection

MA is the largest [[value-based-care]] program in the U.S. by beneficiary count. Because plans receive capitated payments from CMS, every MA plan is inherently operating under a value-based financial structure — they profit by keeping members healthy and managing costs efficiently. This makes MA plan performance, payment policy, and quality measurement central to the broader VBC landscape.
