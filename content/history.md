# History of the U.S. Healthcare System

**Summary**: A chronological account of how the U.S. healthcare system was built — from the pre-Medicare era through the ACA and into the current reform debates. Useful for understanding why the system is structured the way it is.

**Sources**: [A layman's guide to the U.S. health care system](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4193322/) (1992, primary for pre-ACA history), [The Affordable Care Act 101](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/health-policy-101-the-affordable-care-act/), [Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/eight-trends-shaping-2026-healthcare-costs/#Average%20monthly%20health%20insurance%20premiums,%20by%20market%20segment,%202010-2024)

## Before Medicare: A Fragmented Private System (pre-1965)

Before 1965, the U.S. had no major public health insurance programs for most citizens. Private employer-sponsored insurance had grown after World War II, partly because wage controls during the war made health benefits a way to attract workers, and partly because tax policy made employer contributions tax-exempt. But coverage was uneven. In 1963, 75% of adults under 65 had hospital insurance, compared to only 56% of those 65 and older — the elderly were considered high-risk and were often effectively uninsurable in private markets.

Congress debated national health insurance for decades. The first nationwide hospital insurance bill was introduced in 1942 but failed. Debate continued through the 1950s and early 1960s.

## 1965: Medicare and Medicaid

The landmark legislation was enacted in 1965 under President Johnson. **Medicare** and **Medicaid** were a political compromise: rather than universal national health insurance, Congress targeted the groups most at risk — the elderly and the poor. The elderly were broadly uninsurable in the private market; the poor were left out of employer coverage.

- **[[medicare|Medicare]]** initially covered about 19 million elderly people (roughly 10% of the population), nearly doubling the number of insured seniors overnight. In 1972 it was extended to people under 65 with disabilities or end-stage renal disease.
- **[[medicaid|Medicaid]]** initially covered about 10 million people; by 1973 enrollment had grown to 19.6 million.

Both programs originally paid hospitals their costs and physicians their charges — an arrangement that, combined with the sudden expansion of coverage, became a major engine of healthcare cost growth through the 1970s and 1980s.

## 1970s: Cost Explosion and Regulatory Response

The 1970s were defined by rapidly rising healthcare costs and attempts to control them through regulation and planning. Key developments:

- **1974 National Health Planning Act**: Created state and local health planning agencies; states passed certificate-of-need laws to limit investment in hospital and nursing home facilities.
- **1974 ERISA (Employee Retirement Income Security Act)**: Originally intended to regulate pension funds, ERISA pre-empted state regulation of self-insured employer benefit plans. This gave employers a major incentive to self-insure, exempting them from state insurance mandates. The consequences for health policy were profound and lasting.
- The Carter Administration (1977–81) pushed for direct Federal controls on hospital spending, but Congress did not enact them.

## 1980s: Reagan Era — Competition over Regulation

The Reagan administration shifted the intellectual paradigm from regulation to managed competition. The goal was to let market forces contain costs. In practice, reforms were a pragmatic mix:

- **1983: Medicare Prospective Payment System (PPS/DRGs)**: The most significant hospital payment reform of the era. Medicare shifted from paying hospitals their costs (retrospective, cost-based) to a fixed price per admission based on diagnosis (DRGs — diagnosis-related groups). This created incentives for hospitals to reduce length of stay and cut costs. Hospital inpatient admissions declined significantly through the decade.
- **1989: Resource-Based Relative Value Scale (RBRVS)**: Replaced charge-based Medicare physician payment with a system based on the input resources required for each procedure, phased in starting 1992. Also introduced spending targets for physician services.
- **1988: Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act (MCCA)**: The largest expansion of Medicare benefits since 1965 — added outpatient prescription drug coverage, expanded nursing home benefits, and limited out-of-pocket liability. *Repealed just one year later* following a tax revolt by high-income elderly who were required to pay a surcharge to finance the expansion. This episode made Congress reluctant for years to propose benefit expansions funded by taxing current beneficiaries.
- The health planning legislation of the 1970s was repealed in 1986.

**Managed care growth**: [[value-based-care|HMOs and PPOs]] grew substantially during the 1980s. HMO enrollment reached ~37 million by the early 1990s; PPO enrollment grew from 1% to 10% of large employer plan participants between 1986 and 1989.

## 1990s: Managed Care Dominance and Failed Reform

The early 1990s saw managed care become the dominant delivery model and a renewed push for major health reform. The Clinton Administration's Health Security Act (1993) proposed universal coverage through employer mandates and managed competition, but failed to pass Congress. The debate settled the terms that would persist into the 2000s: universal coverage vs. market-based solutions, employer mandates vs. individual choice.

## 2010: The Affordable Care Act

The most significant healthcare legislation since Medicare/Medicaid was signed by President Obama on March 23, 2010. The [[affordable-care-act|ACA]] built on the existing system rather than replacing it:

- **Medicaid expansion** to 138% of the federal poverty level (initially mandated for all states, but made optional by a 2012 Supreme Court ruling)
- **Health Insurance Marketplaces** — regulated exchanges with subsidized coverage for people without employer or public coverage
- **Individual mandate** — requirement to have coverage or pay a penalty (later reduced to $0 in 2019)
- **Consumer protections** — ban on denial for pre-existing conditions, no lifetime limits, coverage of young adults to 26, required essential health benefits
- **Employer mandate** — large employers required to offer affordable coverage or pay penalties

The uninsured rate fell from 14–16% before the ACA to a record low of **7.7% by 2023**.

## 2017: Repeal Attempts and Mandate Elimination

Under President Trump's first term, Republicans attempted multiple times to repeal and replace the ACA. All major repeal efforts failed in the Senate. The only significant structural change was the elimination of the individual mandate penalty (reduced to $0 as part of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, effective 2019).

## 2021: American Rescue Plan — Enhanced ACA Subsidies

The COVID-19 relief package of March 2021 (ARPA) significantly expanded ACA Marketplace subsidies — eliminating the "subsidy cliff" at 400% FPL and making premiums more affordable for low-income enrollees. Enrollment surged to record levels. These enhanced subsidies were extended by the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) through the end of 2025.

## 2025: The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA)

The major domestic policy legislation of 2025 included sweeping healthcare provisions. See [[obbba]] for detail. Key changes:
- Medicaid work/community engagement requirements starting 2027
- Caps on state-directed Medicaid payments
- ACA Marketplace eligibility and verification changes
- $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program
- CBO estimates ~10 million people will become uninsured as a result

## 2026: The Great Healthcare Plan

In January 2026, the Trump administration introduced "The Great Healthcare Plan," urging Congress to lower premiums, reduce drug prices, expand coverage options, and require price transparency from any provider accepting Medicare or Medicaid. As of early 2026 the proposal is a political framework rather than enacted legislation. See [[price-transparency]] and [[pharmacy]].
