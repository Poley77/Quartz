# Value-Based Care

**Summary**: The ongoing shift in U.S. healthcare payment from fee-for-service (volume) to outcome- and quality-based models — still in transition after decades of effort, and becoming a strategic priority rather than a pilot program in 2026.

## The Core Problem with Fee-for-Service

The traditional U.S. payment system pays providers for each service rendered — a visit, a test, a procedure. This **fee-for-service (FFS)** model creates a structural incentive to deliver more care, not better care. More admissions, more tests, and more procedures mean more revenue regardless of patient outcomes.

Studies have documented the consequences. RAND Corporation research found that **15–30% of certain procedures are inappropriate or unnecessary** by clinical evidence standards. Geographic variation in procedure rates is dramatic and cannot be explained by patient population differences — it reflects how FFS incentives interact with local physician practice patterns. (Source: [A layman's guide to the U.S. health care system](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4193322/))

FFS also creates a fragmentation incentive: specialists, hospitals, and primary care physicians are each paid separately for their portion of care, with no payment for coordination between them.

## What Value-Based Care Is

Value-based care (VBC) is an umbrella term for payment models that tie reimbursement to outcomes, quality, or cost efficiency rather than volume. The defining feature is **risk-sharing**: providers bear some or all of the financial consequence when care is poor, costly, or fragmented.

Common models:

| Model | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Pay-for-performance (P4P)** | Bonuses or penalties based on quality metrics (readmissions, preventive care rates, etc.) — still primarily FFS with overlaid incentives |
| **Bundled payments** | Single payment for an episode of care (e.g., a hip replacement plus 90 days of follow-up); providers share the payment and any savings |
| **Shared savings (ACOs)** | Providers form Accountable Care Organizations that are collectively responsible for a defined population; if total costs come in below target, they share the savings with the payer |
| **Capitation / full risk** | Providers receive a fixed payment per member per month to cover all care; they keep the difference if they spend less, absorb the loss if they spend more |

## Historical Roots: HMOs and Managed Care

The predecessors to modern VBC were **Health Maintenance Organizations (HMOs)**, which began in the mid-20th century and grew rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s. HMOs receive a fixed monthly premium per enrollee and must provide a comprehensive benefit package from a defined network of providers — a capitated model.

Studies found HMOs saved **20–30% compared to FFS** primarily by reducing unnecessary hospital admissions. However, concerns about quality and the perception that HMOs denied necessary care led to a consumer backlash in the late 1990s. **Preferred Provider Organizations (PPOs)** offered a middle path: discounted rates within a network, but out-of-network access at higher cost. PPO enrollment grew from 1% of large employer plan participants in 1986 to 10% by 1989.

By the 1990s, HMO enrollment had reached roughly 37 million Americans. The "managed care backlash" of that era — litigation, state mandates, "patients' bill of rights" legislation — slowed the shift and entrenched FFS as the default. The lesson absorbed by policymakers: moving too aggressively toward utilization control without quality safeguards produces political backlash.

## The ACA Era and ACOs

![[VBC Policy Timeline 2003-2023.png]]

The [[affordable-care-act|ACA (2010)]] created the Medicare Shared Savings Program, establishing **Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs)** in Medicare. ACOs are groups of physicians, hospitals, and other providers that voluntarily agree to be held accountable for the cost and quality of care for an assigned Medicare population. If the ACO's total spending comes in below a benchmark, it shares the savings with Medicare; in some tracks, it also bears downside risk for spending above the benchmark.

The Medicare ACO program has grown steadily. It represents the most significant structural move toward population health-based payment in the U.S. public programs.

## 2026 Status: A Strategic Priority, Not a Pilot

As of 2026, value-based care is at an inflection point. Key characteristics:

- Both government and commercial payers are expanding outcome-based reimbursement arrangements
- Fee-for-service models continue to dominate actual volume, but more providers are entering risk-based contracts
- Health systems are beginning to restructure care pathways around **longitudinal patient outcomes** rather than episodic treatment
- 2026 is described as a "junction point" where VBC becomes a strategic operational priority rather than a pilot program (Source: [10 Healthcare Trends Reshaping the US Healthcare in 2026](https://www.americanhhm.com/articles/10-healthcare-trends-reshaping-us-system-2026))
- Continued growth of VBC estimated at **2–4% annually** through 2029 (Source: McKinsey)

**Medicare Advantage** — the privatized Medicare option — is the largest VBC laboratory in the U.S. Plans receive a capitated payment from CMS and must provide all Medicare benefits plus often supplemental benefits, creating strong incentives to keep members healthy and out of hospitals. MA enrolled roughly half of all Medicare beneficiaries by 2024.

## Why the Transition Is Hard

Despite decades of effort, FFS still dominates U.S. healthcare. The barriers are structural:

**Data and analytics gaps**: VBC requires knowing which patients are high-risk before they get sick — and acting proactively. Most health systems are still building the data infrastructure, care coordination platforms, and population health tools required. (Source: Healthcare System Priorities in 2026)

**Dual running costs**: During the transition period, providers simultaneously bear the overhead of FFS billing (needed for revenue today) and the investment in VBC infrastructure (needed for tomorrow). Margins of 1–2% leave little room for this.

**Misaligned incentives within organizations**: Specialist physicians are typically paid on production. A health system in a VBC contract has an incentive to reduce referrals and procedures; the specialist has the opposite incentive. Aligning these requires compensation redesign.

**Risk selection**: Capitated models create incentives to enroll healthier patients and avoid sick ones. Historic HMO experience demonstrated that "favorable selection" — not care efficiency — explained some of the cost savings. Risk adjustment mechanisms (used in Medicare Advantage, for example) attempt to correct for this.

**FFS entrenchment**: The basic billing, coding, contracting, and compliance infrastructure of U.S. healthcare is built around FFS. Rebuilding it is a multi-decade undertaking.

## Performance-Based Contracting in Digital Health (2025)

The shift toward VBC is visible not just in traditional care reimbursement — it's reshaping how purchasers contract for digital health solutions too. PHTI's 2025 State of Digital Health Purchasing survey of 309 decision-makers across health plans, employers, and health systems found that nearly half now use performance- or risk-based contracts for digital health tools:

- **Health plans lead at 75%**, followed by health systems at 61%. Employers have been more cautious — only 14% have adopted performance-based contracts so far.
- Among those using them, the majority place over half of their total digital health contract value at-risk.
- The most common performance metrics are enrollment/registration (86%), patient-reported outcome measures (62%), ROI based on actual cost savings (61%), and plan adherence (61%).
- Of the purchasers not yet using performance-based contracts, 76% of health plans and 84% of health systems say they want to shift to this model.

The primary barrier is not lack of interest — it's execution. The biggest challenges are limited staff time and limited access to data, with most purchasers identifying improvements to outcomes performance thresholds, payment models, and attribution methodology as the highest-priority changes. (Source: PHTI, 2025 State of Digital Health Purchasing)

## The Role of Technology

[[ai-and-technology|AI and data analytics]] are critical enablers of VBC:
- **Predictive analytics** identify high-risk patients before they experience a costly acute event, allowing proactive intervention
- **Care coordination platforms** track patients across settings and flag gaps in care
- **Population health tools** help organizations manage attributed patient panels and meet quality metrics
- The [[obbba|Rural Health Transformation Program's]] explicit funding for alternative payment models and technology signals federal investment in VBC infrastructure
