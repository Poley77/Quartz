# Medicaid

**Summary**: The joint federal-state health insurance program for low-income Americans — and the primary public insurer for long-term care. Currently undergoing the largest restructuring since its creation via the 2025 OBBBA.

**Sources**: `What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond.md`, `Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs.md`, `The Affordable Care Act 101.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-12

---

## How Medicaid Works

Medicaid is jointly financed by the federal government and states. The federal government matches state spending at rates that vary by state income levels — ranging from 50% to 83%, with the federal government covering 90% of the cost for the ACA Medicaid expansion population. States administer the program within broad federal guidelines.

**Who is eligible** (as of 2025):
- Adults with incomes up to **138% of the federal poverty level** in states that expanded Medicaid under the ACA (FPL = $15,650/yr for a single individual in 2025)
- In non-expansion states: generally only people who are elderly, blind, disabled, pregnant, or parents of dependent children below the state-set income level
- Children and pregnant women face more generous income thresholds than adults

**Medicaid expansion**: As of early 2025, **40 states and DC** had expanded Medicaid. The 10 non-expansion states leave an estimated **1.4 million** people in a "coverage gap" — too much income for traditional Medicaid, too little for ACA Marketplace subsidies.

## Scale

Medicaid is the largest health insurer in the U.S. by number of enrollees, covering roughly 1 in 5 Americans. It is the dominant payer for:
- Long-term nursing home care (~43% of all nursing home expenditures)
- Low-income children and pregnant women
- People with disabilities

## Financial Condition in 2024–2026

Medicaid's finances have deteriorated sharply:
- Managed care EBITDA collapsed from $14 billion (2023) to $3 billion (2024), primarily due to adverse selection following the end of pandemic-era continuous enrollment
- EBITDA is expected to go negative in 2025 and 2026 before the [[obbba|OBBBA]] changes even take effect
- The fundamental problem: healthier enrollees have been exiting faster than sicker ones, worsening the risk pool; rate adjustments from states lag 18–24 months behind costs

## The OBBBA and Medicaid's Future

The [[obbba|One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, 2025)]] represents the largest rollback of Medicaid since the program was created. Key provisions taking effect primarily in 2027:

- **Community engagement requirements**: Expansion-eligible adults must verify 80 hours/month of work, education, or other qualified activities, or qualify for an exemption. About **20 million people** will be subject to these requirements.
- **Provider tax changes**: States cannot create new provider taxes or increase existing ones; state-directed payments to hospitals and nursing facilities are capped at 100–110% of Medicare rates for expansion states, reducing provider revenue
- **Federal spending reductions**: CBO estimates $900+ billion reduction in federal Medicaid spending over a decade
- **Expected enrollment impact**: 6–7 million additional disenrollments from managed care organizations in 2027–28, pushing managed care EBITDA to approximately **negative $7 billion** by 2028
- **CBO total**: ~10 million people to become uninsured from all OBBBA healthcare provisions (Medicaid + ACA changes)

States are responding by considering cuts to Medicaid reimbursement rates, limiting benefits (e.g., dental), and implementing new eligibility verification procedures.

## What Happens to Disenrolled Medicaid Members?

McKinsey estimates that of the ~9 million expected to disenroll:
- ~1 million will transition to ACA Marketplace coverage
- ~5.5–6.5 million will work part or full time without employer-sponsored coverage (becoming uninsured)
- ~1–1.5 million will transition to employer-sponsored insurance

## Historical Context

Medicaid was enacted in 1965 alongside [[medicare|Medicare]]. It originally covered about 10 million people. The [[affordable-care-act|ACA (2010)]] dramatically expanded Medicaid's reach — the expansion population (adults 19–64 at up to 138% FPL) added millions of newly eligible people in states that chose to expand. A 2012 Supreme Court ruling made expansion optional, leading to the current patchwork.
