# Top Trends in U.S. Healthcare: 2026

**Summary**: A synthesized view of the major forces reshaping U.S. healthcare in 2026 — drawn from McKinsey, KFF, CBO, AHA, and primary industry sources.

**Sources**: [What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/healthcare/our-insights/what-to-expect-in-us-healthcare), [Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/eight-trends-shaping-2026-healthcare-costs/#Average%20monthly%20health%20insurance%20premiums,%20by%20market%20segment,%202010-2024), Healthcare System Priorities in 2026, [10 Healthcare Trends Reshaping the US Healthcare in 2026](https://www.americanhhm.com/articles/10-healthcare-trends-reshaping-us-system-2026), [Fast Facts on U.S. Hospitals, 2026](https://www.aha.org/statistics/fast-facts-us-hospitals), [The U.S. Health Care System in 2026: Inside the Great Healthcare Plan](https://www.biobrain.io/blog/the-u-s-health-care-system-in-2026-inside-the-great-healthcare-plan)

**Last updated**: 2026-05-12

## 1. AI Transitions from Pilot to Operational Infrastructure

Artificial intelligence has crossed the threshold from experiment to core operations in U.S. healthcare. 85% of healthcare organizations are now pursuing or have deployed generative AI solutions (McKinsey). Ambient scribing — AI that listens to patient-clinician conversations and auto-generates clinical notes — is the most mature application, used by an estimated 10%+ of U.S. physicians with documented 60–70% reductions in documentation time.

**Insight**: The pattern matches classic technology S-curves. Early adopters proved ROI; the majority are now deploying. But AI is not neutral — it is creating a new billing risk: AI documentation tools are systematically increasing the proportion of visits coded at higher complexity levels, raising billing amounts independent of patient health. Payers are responding with their own AI-driven utilization management, setting up an adversarial AI arms race that will put upward pressure on total spending. The technology solves one problem (burnout) while creating another (cost inflation through coding intensity).

See [[ai-and-technology]], [[workforce]].

## 2. The OBBBA Triggers the Largest Coverage Disruption Since the ACA

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2025) cut $900 billion from Medicaid over a decade, added work requirements for 20 million expansion enrollees, and restructured ACA Marketplace subsidies. The CBO estimates ~10 million Americans will become uninsured as a result. This is the biggest single policy shock to healthcare coverage in over a decade.

**Insight**: The distribution of impact is highly uneven. Medicaid managed care plans face EBITDA projected at negative $7 billion by 2028. Safety-net hospitals in high-Medicaid markets face simultaneous reimbursement cuts and surging uncompensated care. Meanwhile, the $50B Rural Health Transformation Program is real money for technology and infrastructure — but it is temporary and does not offset the permanent loss of Medicaid funding. States that act quickly and submit strong plans will capture disproportionate transformation funds; states that move slowly will receive less while absorbing the same benefit cuts.

See [[obbba]], [[medicaid]], [[uninsured]].

## 3. Drug Spending Accelerates — GLP-1s Lead, Specialty Drugs Follow

Drug net spending rose 11% in 2024 — faster than any other healthcare category. GLP-1 agonists (Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro) alone drove roughly half the increase. U.S. gross drug spending is projected to reach $990 billion by 2029. Specialty biologics — now 54% of net drug spending, up from 43% in 2019 — are reshaping the economics of pharmacy, hospital outpatient departments, and insurer benefit design.

**Insight**: The GLP-1 boom is both a cost crisis and a potential long-term opportunity. These drugs reduce obesity, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes — conditions that are among the most expensive chronic diseases to manage. The key tension is temporal: the costs are immediate, while downstream savings from better chronic disease management play out over decades. Employers and plans making coverage decisions today have no clean way to capture those future savings. Direct-to-consumer models and oral formulations may eventually lower GLP-1 prices, but not on a timeline that helps current payers. Meanwhile, PBM reform (Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026) is beginning to shift the incentive structure, though its effects on list prices remain to be seen.

See [[pharmacy]], [[healthcare-costs]].

## 4. Workforce Burnout Reaches Crisis Level — and AI Scribing Is the First Real Fix

More than half of U.S. clinicians report burnout symptoms. Physicians spend roughly two hours on documentation for every one hour of direct patient care. Structural shortages in primary care and rural medicine are compounding attrition — health systems are filling gaps with travel nurses at 3x normal rates. In a 1–2% margin environment, this is existential.

**Insight**: Workforce is the most urgent operational problem in healthcare, but it is also the one where near-term intervention is actually working. AI ambient scribing has unusually clear ROI — less documentation time directly reduces the top driver of burnout. Unlike many AI applications that require complex integration or long implementation cycles, scribing tools can be layered onto existing EHR workflows. The strategic insight: health systems that move fastest on ambient scribing will gain a meaningful recruitment and retention advantage in a tight labor market. The deeper workforce shortage (too few physicians in training, specialty-primary imbalance) is a decade-long problem with no quick fix.

See [[workforce]], [[ai-and-technology]], [[rural-health]].

## 5. Industry Margins Compress — Financial Stress Is System-Wide

Industry EBITDA as a percentage of national health expenditure fell from 11.2% (2019) to 8.9% (2024), and is projected to reach 8.7% by 2027. Most large hospital systems operate on 1–2% operating margins. At the same time, healthcare costs are the #1 household financial worry for Americans — above food, rent, and utilities (KFF).

**Insight**: The U.S. healthcare system is caught in a structural bind: providers are under severe margin pressure while the people they serve cannot afford to pay more. The system is simultaneously too expensive for patients and insufficiently profitable for providers. This is not a short-term squeeze — it reflects a structural inability to control costs within a fragmented FFS system. The organizations best positioned to survive are those that can either (a) achieve genuine cost reductions through technology and value-based contracts or (b) consolidate to gain pricing power. Both paths are playing out simultaneously, which creates the paradox of efficiency-seeking on one side and price inflation from market power on the other.

See [[healthcare-costs]], [[hospitals]], [[value-based-care]].

## 6. Value-Based Care Reaches an Inflection Point

After decades as a pilot program, value-based care is becoming a strategic operational priority. Payers and providers are both expanding risk-sharing arrangements. Medicare Advantage — a capitated program — now enrolls roughly half of all Medicare beneficiaries. VBC is projected to grow at 2–4% annually through 2029, and is described in the literature as reaching a "junction point" where transformation is no longer optional.

**Insight**: The transition is structurally difficult because it requires providers to simultaneously maintain fee-for-service billing (for current revenue) while building VBC infrastructure (for future survival). On 1–2% margins, few organizations have capacity to fund both. AI and predictive analytics are the critical enabler — VBC only works when you can identify high-risk patients before they get sick. The organizations most likely to succeed are those that have already made the data and analytics investments, or that partner with health services technology vendors who can provide that infrastructure. The OBBBA's work requirements also create a new VBC challenge: the churn in Medicaid enrollment makes population health management nearly impossible.

See [[value-based-care]], [[ai-and-technology]], [[obbba]].

## 7. Site of Care Shifts Away from Hospitals

More than half of patient encounters now occur in outpatient settings. Ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs), urgent care, home health, and virtual care are growing consistently. ASC EBITDA margins are ~24% — dramatically higher than the 6.8% of acute inpatient hospitals. Hospitals' share of overall provider profits is expected to decline from 41% (2019) to 38% (2029).

**Insight**: This trend is structural and will not reverse. Payers want lower-cost settings; patients prefer convenience; new procedures have been cleared for outpatient performance that historically required inpatient stays (orthopedic, cardiovascular). The problem for hospitals is dual: they are losing commercially insured volume — their most profitable business — to ASCs, while retaining complex, sicker, or uninsured patients in expensive inpatient beds. The strategic imperative for health systems is to own the ambulatory assets before competitors do, but capital is scarce on 1–2% margins. Systems that delay risk having their outpatient volume captured by retail chains (CVS, Amazon) or PE-backed specialty groups.

See [[hospitals]], [[value-based-care]], [[healthcare-consolidation]].

## 8. Market Consolidation Deepens — and Faces Its Strongest Regulatory Pushback Yet

70% of community hospitals are now part of a health system. One or two health systems control all inpatient commercial care in roughly half of U.S. metro areas. Vertical integration is accelerating — UnitedHealth/Optum now combines an insurer, physician network, PBM, and data analytics firm. In 2026, bipartisan legislative efforts to restrict anti-competitive contracting and limit vertical integration are gaining traction.

**Insight**: The research consensus on consolidation is now firm: it reliably raises prices, and the evidence on quality improvement is weak. The regulatory response is arriving late — the consolidation happened over 30 years and is hard to unwind. The most practically impactful near-term policy is not unwinding past mergers but restricting the contracting practices that consolidated systems use to maintain their pricing power (all-or-nothing contracting, anti-tiering clauses). Price transparency is a necessary but insufficient complement: it exposes the size of the price premium but doesn't help patients in markets where there is no lower-cost alternative.

See [[healthcare-consolidation]], [[price-transparency]], [[healthcare-costs]].

## 9. Price Transparency Moves Toward Action

Since 2021, hospitals and health plans have been required to publish machine-readable files of negotiated rates. The data is now available but hard to use. In 2026, the Great Healthcare Plan, the Patients Deserve Price Tags Act, and major insurer endorsement signal political momentum for the next phase: accessible, actionable price data.

**Insight**: The literature suggests that transparency alone does not lower prices — it requires competitive markets to function as a discipline mechanism. Where markets are already competitive, transparency helps. Where a dominant system controls the market, knowing their prices are high doesn't help patients who have no alternatives. The more impactful near-term use of price data is by self-insured employers and large insurers who can act on it in network design. One underappreciated dynamic: price transparency is exposing the extent to which facility fees and hospital outpatient pricing far exceed ASC rates for identical procedures — data that is strengthening the case for site neutrality policies that hospitals deeply oppose.

See [[price-transparency]], [[healthcare-consolidation]], [[hospitals]].

## 10. Health Equity Shifts from Reporting to Action

Persistent disparities in health outcomes across racial, income, and geographic lines are being increasingly addressed as operational problems rather than measurement exercises. Healthcare organizations are integrating social determinants of health (SDOH) data into care workflows and risk stratification. Payment models are beginning to include equity objectives. CMS has added equity measures to several Medicare quality programs.

**Insight**: The OBBBA creates a significant headwind for health equity. Medicaid work requirements will disproportionately affect low-income people of color, who face higher barriers to documenting compliance. ACA navigator funding cuts reduce support for populations that most depend on enrollment assistance. The policy environment is pulling in opposite directions: CMS pushing equity-oriented payment models while legislation reduces coverage for the populations those models are meant to serve. Organizations committed to equity will need to be deliberate about data: measuring outcomes by race, income, and ZIP code — and demonstrating improvement — as insurers and CMS increasingly require it as a condition of payment.

See [[health-equity]], [[obbba]], [[medicaid]], [[uninsured]].

## Cross-Cutting Themes

Reading across these trends, three deeper patterns emerge:

**The AI paradox**: AI simultaneously solves healthcare's workforce crisis (scribing, automation) and worsens its cost crisis (coding inflation). The net effect will depend on how quickly payers build countervailing AI controls, and whether clinical AI translates into fewer unnecessary procedures.

**The coverage unwind**: The OBBBA is not just a policy change — it's the reversal of a 15-year coverage expansion. Its downstream effects will interact with every other trend: more uncompensated care for hospitals, disrupted population health programs, worsened health equity outcomes, and increased ED utilization.

**Winners and losers are diverging**: The HST sector is growing at 8%+ annually. ASCs are earning 24% EBITDA margins. Meanwhile, hospitals are at 6.8% and Medicaid managed care is heading negative. The healthcare industry is not uniformly stressed — it is bifurcating between the parts that are structurally favored by current trends and the parts that are not.
