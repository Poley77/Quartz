# The Strategy That Will Fix Health Care

**Summary**: Michael Porter and Thomas Lee's foundational 2013 HBR argument for outcome-based care — the clearest articulation of why the U.S. healthcare system fails and what the structural fix looks like.

**Sources**: Porter & Lee, Harvard Business Review, "The Strategy That Will Fix Health Care" (October 2013)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## The Central Argument

Porter and Lee argue that the U.S. healthcare system's problems — high cost, poor outcomes, fragmentation, waste — all stem from a single root cause: **providers don't compete on value**. They compete on volume, on service lines, on amenity, on reputation. But almost never on who actually delivers better health outcomes per dollar spent.

The reason: **outcomes are not measured**. Because no one tracks whether patients with knee replacement surgery at Hospital A do better than at Hospital B, patients and referring physicians cannot make value-based decisions. Without that information, competition gravitates toward cost, convenience, reputation, and bedside manner — which are visible — rather than health outcomes, which are not.

The fix is not more regulation, more insurance coverage, or more spending. It is restructuring how providers compete — around **patient value**, defined as health outcomes per dollar of cost.

## The Six-Part Agenda

Porter and Lee lay out six interconnected changes needed to make value-based competition possible:

### 1. Organize Around Medical Conditions, Not Specialties

Most health systems are organized by department: cardiology, orthopedics, oncology, neurology. This fragments care across the patient journey — a patient with heart failure sees a cardiologist, an internist, a nephrologist, and a pharmacist, but no one is accountable for the whole.

The alternative is **Integrated Practice Units (IPUs)** — multidisciplinary teams organized around a full cycle of care for a specific condition. An IPU for type 2 diabetes would include an endocrinologist, a primary care physician, a diabetes educator, a dietitian, a care manager, and a pharmacist, all operating under shared accountability for outcomes across the full care cycle from diagnosis through ongoing management.

IPUs are the organizational form that makes the other five changes possible. Without them, outcome measurement, bundled payment, and care integration are impossible to implement.

### 2. Measure Outcomes and Costs for Every Patient

This is the hardest part and the most essential. Porter and Lee identify two sets of measurements that need to happen:

**Outcome measurement**: Track what actually matters to patients — functional outcomes, survival, recovery time, recurrence rates, complication rates — not just process metrics ("did we order the test?"). These outcomes need to be measured consistently, risk-adjusted, and compared across providers.

**Cost measurement**: Track the actual cost of delivering a full cycle of care, not just what is billed. Time-driven activity-based costing (TDABC) allows organizations to understand the real cost inputs at each step, identifying waste and enabling true cost comparison.

The reason this hasn't happened: it requires infrastructure investment, it creates transparency that exposes variation in quality, and it requires agreement on outcome definitions that providers often resist. Where it has been done — Cleveland Clinic's Outcomes Book, international patient-reported outcomes programs — the results have driven quality improvement.

### 3. Move to Bundled Payments for Care Cycles

[[Fee-for-service]] pays for each service separately, fragmenting accountability. No single entity is responsible for the full outcome of a hip replacement, a cancer treatment, or a diabetic patient's long-term health.

**Bundled payments** hold a single entity accountable for the full cycle of care for a condition — from diagnosis through the primary treatment through any complications within a defined recovery period. A single bundled payment for a hip replacement would cover the surgery, the hospital stay, rehabilitation, physical therapy, and any readmissions within 90 days.

This forces the accountability that IPU organization needs to function: if one entity receives one payment for the full cycle, it has every reason to coordinate, communicate, and optimize the handoffs. CMS has piloted this through the Bundled Payments for Care Improvement (BPCI) program, with mixed but promising results.

### 4. Integrate Care Across Separate Facilities

High-value care requires more than getting the clinical team right inside one facility. It requires managing transitions across facilities — from acute hospital to rehab to outpatient follow-up to primary care — where most breakdowns happen.

Porter and Lee call for "hub and spoke" delivery systems where specialized centers of excellence handle complex cases, while lower-cost community facilities handle routine follow-up. This is fundamentally at odds with the fragmented, hospital-centric structure of most U.S. health systems, where every facility wants to do everything.

### 5. Expand Geographic Reach to Excellence

The logic here is counterintuitive: rather than having mediocre capability everywhere, build exceptional capability in fewer places and route patients to where care is best.

High-volume specialized centers develop better outcomes through experience and learning effects. A cardiac surgery center doing 1,000 cases per year has substantially better survival rates than one doing 200 cases per year. The same pattern holds for cancer surgery, complex joint replacement, and many other procedures.

The policy and political resistance is significant — every community wants its own hospital to do everything — but the quality and cost case for selective concentration of complex care is strong.

### 6. Build an Enabling IT Platform

All five of the above changes require data: outcome data, cost data, patient data across the full care cycle. The current generation of healthcare IT — largely built for billing and documentation rather than outcomes measurement and care coordination — is insufficient.

The enabling platform needs to: track outcomes longitudinally across encounters and facilities, support care team communication across the IPU, measure cost at the activity level, and allow benchmarking and comparison across providers.

Porter and Lee wrote this in 2013 when EHR adoption was just becoming widespread. The infrastructure has improved, but the outcome measurement and cost transparency components remain the weakest links in most health systems' IT strategies.

## Why This Framework Matters in 2026

The Porter/Lee framework is now 13 years old, but it remains the clearest theoretical foundation for what [[value-based-care]] is trying to accomplish. Many of the programs and models that followed map directly to this agenda:

| Porter/Lee Element | 2026 Implementation |
|---|---|
| IPUs | ACO care teams, medical homes, specialty care coordination programs |
| Outcome measurement | HEDIS, Star Ratings, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) |
| Bundled payments | BPCI Advanced, CMS episode payment models |
| Care integration | ACO networks, care transition programs, hospital-post-acute partnerships |
| Geographic concentration | Centers of Excellence programs (major insurers), preferred provider designations |
| Enabling IT | Population health platforms, AI-assisted coding, ambient scribing |

The framework is also useful **diagnostically**: when VBC programs underperform, it's often because they've implemented one or two elements — shared savings, some quality metrics — without the full structural change (IPUs, outcome measurement, cost transparency) that makes the system actually work. Shared savings without outcome measurement creates incentives to reduce cost by rationing rather than improving quality.

## The Unfinished Business

As of 2026, the Porter/Lee agenda remains largely unfinished:

- **Outcome measurement** is still primarily process-based (did we order the screening?) rather than outcome-based (did the patient get better?)
- **Cost transparency** remains nearly nonexistent — patients and referring physicians cannot compare the actual cost of a procedure across facilities
- **IPUs** are the exception, not the rule — most specialty care is still organized by department rather than patient condition
- **Bundled payments** account for a small fraction of total payment volume

The vision is right; the implementation is partial. The constraint is not knowledge — the framework is well understood — but the incumbent interests and capital requirements involved in restructuring the delivery system around it.
