# Medicare

**Summary**: The federal health insurance program for adults 65 and older, the disabled, and people with end-stage renal disease — and the single largest health insurer in the country.

**Sources**: `What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond.md`, `The Affordable Care Act 101.md`, `A layman's guide to the U.S. health care system.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## Structure

Medicare has four parts:

| Part | Covers | Financed by |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Inpatient hospital, limited skilled nursing facility, some home health | Payroll tax (1.45% employee + 1.45% employer) |
| **B** | Physician services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment | Premiums (~25%) + general federal revenues (~75%) |
| **C** | Medicare Advantage — private plans that replace A+B | Capitated federal payment to plan |
| **D** | Prescription drugs | Premiums + federal subsidies |

## Scale and Enrollment

Medicare is the largest single health insurer in the country:

- **67.6 million** total beneficiaries as of 2024
- **~34.4 million** enrolled in Medicare Advantage (Part C) — roughly 55% of Medicare
- **~30 million** remaining in Traditional Medicare (Parts A and B)
- **~57 million** have prescription drug coverage through Part D

Medicare now represents approximately **13% of the entire federal budget** — roughly **$910 billion in FY2024** — making it the second-largest federal expenditure after Social Security. It is also the single largest purchaser of healthcare services in the U.S. This scale means that Medicare payment policy shapes the entire industry: when CMS changes how it pays hospitals, or creates new quality requirements, the effects cascade throughout the system.

## The Solvency Question

Medicare's financing is under long-term strain. The Hospital Insurance (HI) Trust Fund, which finances Part A, is funded by payroll taxes. As the population ages, the ratio of workers paying into the fund versus beneficiaries drawing from it is shrinking.

**Depletion timeline**:
- **2033**: Medicare Trustees' projected depletion date for the HI Trust Fund (2024 Annual Report)
- **2040**: Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projection (slightly more optimistic due to different economic assumptions)

After depletion, the Trust Fund would only be able to pay approximately **89% of scheduled Part A benefits** from ongoing payroll tax revenues — a mandatory 11% cut to hospital payments unless Congress acts.

Parts B and D are financed differently — through premiums and general revenues — and cannot technically "run out" in the same way, but they impose rising costs on the federal budget over time.

**What could prevent depletion**:
- Payroll tax increases (politically difficult)
- Benefit reductions or eligibility changes (e.g., raising the Medicare eligibility age from 65 to 67)
- Provider payment rate cuts
- Drug pricing reforms that reduce per-beneficiary costs
- Structural shift to more efficient care delivery (VBC, ACOs)

The solvency question has driven much of Medicare policy for decades — it is a major political constraint on benefit expansion and a major motivation behind value-based care initiatives.

## Medicare Advantage (Part C)

Medicare Advantage is the privatized alternative to traditional Medicare. Beneficiaries enroll in a private plan that receives a capitated payment from CMS based on demographics and [[risk-adjustment-coding|risk adjustment]]. Plans often offer supplemental benefits (dental, vision, OTC products) that traditional Medicare does not cover.

**2026 status**:
- MA enrollment reached **34.4 million** in 2025 — 55% of all Medicare beneficiaries
- Growth has moderated from 8–9% annually (2019–2024) to an expected 4–5% through 2029
- About **72% of MA plans operated at negative EBITDA** in 2024, driven by lower CMS rate increases, tighter risk adjustment (V28 model transition), and a ~7% rise in medical costs
- Margins expected to begin gradual recovery in 2026 (100–150 basis points improvement)
- The full transition to the **V28 HCC model** completes in 2026, recalibrating risk scores and reducing payments for some conditions

The MA market is also subject to [[taking-stock-medicare-advantage-payment|ongoing debate about overpayment]]: MedPAC estimates MA plans receive $600–$1,000+ more per enrollee per year than traditional Medicare would spend on the same people.

## Demographic Pressures

The aging population is the dominant long-term driver of Medicare costs:
- Population aged 80 and above grew from 3.8% of the U.S. in 2017 to **4.2% in 2024**, projected to reach **5.2% by 2029** (U.S. Census Bureau)
- The 80+ age group spends **$1,673 per member per month** — the highest of any age cohort — growing at 4.8% annually
- This demographic shift adds an estimated **0.5–1.0% to annual claims costs** regardless of other trends

The baby boomer generation (born 1946–1964) is fully in their 60s and 70s now, with the largest cohort entering Medicare over the decade ahead.

## Historical Context

Medicare was enacted in 1965 alongside [[medicaid|Medicaid]] as a political compromise: rather than universal national insurance, coverage was targeted at the elderly (who were effectively uninsurable in private markets) and the poor. The program initially paid hospitals their costs and physicians their charges — a decision that contributed to rapid healthcare cost inflation through the 1970s and 1980s.

Two major payment reforms reshaped Medicare economics:
- **1983**: Prospective Payment System (DRGs) — hospitals now receive a fixed payment per admission based on diagnosis, not actual costs. This ended cost-plus reimbursement for hospitals.
- **1992**: Resource-Based Relative Value Scale (RBRVS) — physician payment based on input resources rather than historical charges.

Key milestones since:
- **1997**: Balanced Budget Act created the Medicare+Choice program (precursor to MA) and set a sustainable growth rate (SGR) for physician payment — a formula that proved unworkable and was permanently replaced in 2015
- **2003**: Medicare Modernization Act added Part D prescription drug coverage — the largest expansion of Medicare since its founding
- **2010**: ACA phased out the Part D "donut hole," added preventive benefits, cut payment growth rates for hospitals and MA plans, and created the Medicare Shared Savings Program (the first ACO program)
- **2022**: Inflation Reduction Act capped out-of-pocket Part D costs at $2,000/year (taking effect 2025) and gave Medicare authority to negotiate drug prices for the first time

## What Medicare Does Not Cover

Traditional Medicare is oriented toward acute care and does not cover:
- **Long-term nursing home care** (beyond short post-acute stays of up to 100 days)
- **Routine dental, vision, and hearing** — major gaps for the elderly population (Medicare Advantage plans often fill these)
- **Custodial home care** (assistance with daily living activities)
- **Most outpatient prescription drugs** in traditional Medicare (covered by Part D, added in 2006)

About **68% of traditional Medicare beneficiaries** hold supplemental ("Medigap") coverage through former employers or self-purchased to fill cost-sharing gaps. The absence of a long-term care benefit is a major policy gap — most elderly Americans face catastrophic costs for nursing home care not covered by Medicare, often spending down to Medicaid eligibility.

## Medicare and OBBBA

The [[obbba|One Big Beautiful Budget Act]] of 2025 has limited direct Medicare cuts — Medicare reduction is politically difficult — but does affect the program indirectly:
- Reduction in Medicaid funding increases the number of Medicaid-to-Medicare dual eligible beneficiaries who may lose Medicaid coverage, creating more cost exposure for them
- Proposed changes to Medicare Advantage oversight and quality reporting
- Continued negotiation of drug prices under IRA authority proceeds
