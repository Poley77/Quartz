# Value-Based Care: What It Is and Why It's Needed

**Summary**: A foundational explainer on why the U.S. moved toward value-based care — what's broken about fee-for-service, and what VBC is trying to fix.

**Sources**: Commonwealth Fund, "Value-Based Care: What It Is, and Why It's Needed" (2023)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## The Problem VBC Is Solving

The U.S. spends more on healthcare than any other high-income country — roughly 18% of GDP — yet ranks near the bottom on health outcomes among peers. Life expectancy is lower. Infant mortality is higher. Chronic disease burdens are greater. The root cause isn't lack of effort or talent. It's the payment model.

Under [[fee-for-service]], providers get paid for each service they deliver: a visit, a test, a procedure. More services mean more revenue, regardless of whether the patient gets better. This creates a system that is structurally incentivized to treat illness rather than prevent it, and to fragment care rather than coordinate it.

The consequences are well-documented:

- **Fragmented care**: Specialists, hospitals, and primary care physicians are each paid separately, with no payment for coordination between them. A patient managing diabetes, heart disease, and kidney disease may see half a dozen specialists with no one accountable for the whole picture.
- **Unnecessary services**: Studies estimate 15–30% of certain procedures are inappropriate or of uncertain benefit by clinical evidence standards (RAND Corporation).
- **Misaligned incentives**: An empty hospital bed is a financial problem, even if it means the community is healthier. Keeping a patient out of the hospital costs the hospital revenue.
- **Prevention not rewarded**: A 30-minute care coordination call that prevents a $30,000 hospitalization generates minimal FFS revenue. The hospitalization generates a lot.

## What Value-Based Care Is

[[Value-based-care|Value-based care (VBC)]] ties reimbursement to outcomes, quality, or cost efficiency rather than volume. Instead of paying per visit or procedure, payers hold providers accountable for the health of a defined patient population.

The defining feature is **risk-sharing**: if care is high-quality and costs come in below a target, providers share in the savings. If costs run over or quality is poor, providers bear some of that financial consequence.

Common VBC payment models:

| Model | Description | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| Pay-for-performance | FFS plus quality bonuses/penalties | Low |
| Shared savings (ACOs) | Providers share savings vs. spending benchmark | Medium |
| Bundled payments | Single payment for an episode of care | Medium |
| Capitation | Fixed PMPM payment; provider bears all cost risk | High |

This realigns the incentives:

- Prevention becomes profitable, not just the right thing to do
- Care coordination is rewarded rather than treated as overhead
- Keeping patients out of the hospital saves money instead of costing revenue
- Catching a disease early is worth more than treating it late

## Why It's Needed Now

90% of U.S. healthcare spending goes toward people with chronic conditions — diabetes, heart disease, COPD, depression. These aren't acute, one-time problems. They require continuous management, coordination, and prevention over years or decades.

Fee-for-service was designed for a different era — acute care, episodic treatment. A patient breaks a bone, a surgeon fixes it, the patient pays. It was never built to handle the chronic disease burden that now dominates American healthcare. VBC is the structural response to that mismatch.

The scale of the gap it's trying to close:

- The U.S. spends ~**$14,500 per person per year** on healthcare — roughly double the OECD average
- Yet U.S. life expectancy (**76.4 years** in 2024) is lower than in 40+ other countries
- The Commonwealth Fund consistently ranks the U.S. last among peer nations on measures of equity, access, and care outcomes despite highest spending

## VBC in Practice: What Actually Changes

For a **primary care physician** in a VBC arrangement, the job changes in concrete ways:
- A monthly care management fee creates revenue for calling a patient who hasn't come in — instead of only getting paid when they show up sick
- A high-risk patient panel is flagged by analytics tools before problems become hospitalizations
- Care gaps (a patient due for a diabetes screening, a blood pressure medication that hasn't been refilled) are surfaced before the visit
- Quality metrics — A1C levels, readmission rates, preventive screening completion — affect compensation

For a **Medicare Advantage plan**, the model is fully capitated: the plan receives a fixed monthly payment from CMS per enrollee and must provide all Medicare benefits from that budget. Every hospitalization, every expensive specialist, every avoidable readmission costs money. Keeping members healthy is the entire financial model.

## Where VBC Stands in 2026

VBC has moved from pilot program to operational infrastructure, but the transition is uneven:

- **55% of Medicare beneficiaries** are now enrolled in Medicare Advantage (fully capitated)
- **53% of Traditional Medicare beneficiaries** are in some accountable care relationship
- 14.3 million beneficiaries are in Medicare Shared Savings Program ACOs
- CMS has set a target for 100% of Medicare beneficiaries to be in accountable care arrangements by 2030

The transition is real but not complete. FFS still dominates total payment volume in commercial insurance. Most providers are running VBC infrastructure and FFS billing simultaneously — a major operational and financial burden.
