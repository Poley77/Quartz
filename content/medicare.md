# Medicare

**Summary**: The federal health insurance program for adults 65 and older, the disabled, and people with end-stage renal disease — and the single largest health insurer in the country.

**Sources**: [What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/healthcare/our-insights/what-to-expect-in-us-healthcare), [The Affordable Care Act 101](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/health-policy-101-the-affordable-care-act/), [A layman's guide to the U.S. health care system](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4193322/) (historical context)

**Last updated**: 2026-05-12

## Historical Context

Medicare was enacted in 1965 alongside [[medicaid|Medicaid]] as a compromise — rather than universal national insurance, coverage was targeted at the elderly (who were effectively uninsurable in private markets) and the poor. The program initially paid hospitals their costs and physicians their charges, contributing to rapid healthcare cost inflation in the 1970s.

Two major payment reforms reshaped Medicare economics:
- **1983**: Prospective Payment System (DRGs) — hospitals now receive a fixed payment per admission based on diagnosis, not actual costs
- **1992**: Resource-Based Relative Value Scale (RBRVS) — physician payment based on input resources, not historical charges

The [[affordable-care-act|ACA (2010)]] made additional changes: phased out the Part D "donut hole," added preventive benefits without cost-sharing, cut growth in payments to hospitals and Medicare Advantage plans, and created the Medicare Shared Savings Program (ACO program).

## Structure

Medicare has four parts:

| Part | Covers | Financed by |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Inpatient hospital, limited skilled nursing facility, some home health | Payroll tax (1.45% employee + 1.45% employer) |
| **B** | Physician services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment | Premiums (~25%) + general federal revenues (~75%) |
| **C** | Medicare Advantage — private plans that replace A+B | Capitated federal payment to plan |
| **D** | Prescription drugs | Premiums + federal subsidies |

## Medicare Advantage (Part C)

[[value-based-care|Medicare Advantage]] is the privatized alternative to traditional Medicare. Beneficiaries enroll in a private plan that receives a capitated payment from CMS based on demographics and risk adjustment. Plans often offer supplemental benefits (dental, vision, OTC products) that traditional Medicare does not cover.

**2026 status**:
- MA enrollment grew 8–9% annually from 2019 to 2024, but is expected to moderate to 4–5% annually through 2029 (Source: McKinsey)
- About 72% of MA plans operated at negative EBITDA in 2024, driven by lower CMS rate increases, tighter risk adjustment policies, and a ~7% rise in medical costs
- Margins expected to begin gradual recovery in 2026 (100–150 basis points) and return to ~2% long-term average by 2029
- Dual-eligible managed care (people with both Medicare and Medicaid) expected to grow 2–3% annually through 2029

## Demographic Pressures

The aging population is the dominant long-term driver of Medicare costs:
- Population aged 80 and above grew from 3.8% of the U.S. in 2017 to 4.2% in 2024, and is projected to reach 5.2% by 2029 (U.S. Census Bureau)
- The 80+ age group spends $1,673 per member per month — the highest of any age cohort — and is growing at 4.8% per year
- This demographic shift is expected to add 0.5–1.0% to annual claims costs

## What Medicare Does Not Cover

Traditional Medicare is oriented toward acute care and does not cover:
- Long-term nursing home care (beyond short post-acute stays)
- Routine dental, vision, and hearing
- Most outpatient prescription drugs (covered by Part D, added in 2006)

About 68% of Medicare beneficiaries hold supplemental ("Medigap") coverage from former employers or self-purchased to fill cost-sharing gaps.
