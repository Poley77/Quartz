# Intro to Value-Based Care

**Summary**: A business model lens on VBC — why two fundamentally different healthcare systems are operating in parallel right now, and why one is winning.

**Sources**: Pearcalls, ["The Healthcare Business Model That Will Define the Next Decade"](https://www.pearcalls.com/blog/intro-to-value-based-care) (2025)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## Two Systems, Same Buildings

There are currently two healthcare systems operating in the U.S. They use the same hospitals, the same doctors, the same EMRs — but run on fundamentally different rules and create completely different incentives.

One is [[fee-for-service]]: get paid per procedure, per visit, per admission. Volume drives revenue.

The other is [[value-based-care]]: get paid for keeping a population healthy. Outcomes drive revenue.

One is shrinking. The other is quietly taking over. More than half of Medicare patients are already in value-based arrangements, and CMS has set a goal for all Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries to be in accountable care arrangements by 2030.

## Why Fee-for-Service Fails Chronic Care

For most of the 20th century, FFS worked reasonably well — healthcare was about fixing acute problems. You got sick, you saw a doctor, they treated you, you got better.

But 90% of the nation's $4.5 trillion in annual healthcare expenditures now go to people with chronic conditions. FFS wasn't built for this world:

- **Prevention doesn't pay.** A 30-minute conversation preventing diabetes complications might bill at $150. The eventual amputation bills at $50,000. The incentive is backward.
- **Coordination isn't rewarded.** When a patient sees five specialists, nobody gets paid to make sure they're aligned. The primary care physician who spends hours coordinating care does so essentially for free.
- **Volume trumps value.** An empty hospital bed is a financial problem even if it means the community is healthier.

## How VBC Flips the Incentives

Under VBC, providers take financial responsibility for a population's health outcomes. Keep patients healthy and costs down — the provider shares in the savings. Let preventable problems become expensive crises — the provider bears some of the cost.

This changes every incentive:

- Prevention becomes profitable
- Care coordination becomes essential, not optional overhead
- Hospital readmissions cost money instead of generating revenue
- Patients and providers become teammates rather than adversaries

## The Mechanics

**Shared savings**: Providers continue receiving FFS payments but also receive a spending target based on historical costs. If total spending comes in below that target, they keep a percentage of the savings (typically 40–75%). This is how the Medicare Shared Savings Program ([[compare-aco-reach-mssp|MSSP]]) works.

**Capitation**: Providers receive a fixed amount per patient per month to cover all care. Profit comes from keeping patients healthy. This is the model used in [[medicare|Medicare Advantage]] and advanced ACO programs like ACO REACH.

**Quality metrics**: Cost reduction alone isn't enough. VBC contracts include extensive quality benchmarks — Star Ratings in Medicare Advantage, HEDIS measures, patient experience scores — that determine whether providers share in savings and by how much.

## Why Now

Three forces converged to make modern VBC viable:

1. **Data infrastructure**: Electronic health records and claims data now allow providers to track population health, identify high-risk patients, and intervene proactively.
2. **Regulatory push**: CMS has systematically expanded ACO and Medicare Advantage programs, creating the payment architecture VBC requires.
3. **Market momentum**: Over 450 ACOs in MSSP serve 11M+ patients. ~40% of all U.S. healthcare payments now flow through value-based contracts. This is no longer a pilot program.
