# Value-Based Care: What It Is and Why It's Needed

**Summary**: A foundational explainer on why the U.S. moved toward value-based care — what's broken about fee-for-service, and what VBC is trying to fix.

**Sources**: Commonwealth Fund, ["Value-Based Care: What It Is, and Why It's Needed"](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/explainer/2023/feb/value-based-care-what-it-is-why-its-needed) (2023)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## The Problem VBC Is Solving

The U.S. spends more on healthcare than any other high-income country — roughly 18% of GDP — yet ranks near the bottom on health outcomes among peers. The root cause isn't lack of effort or talent. It's the payment model.

Under [[fee-for-service]], providers get paid for each service they deliver: a visit, a test, a procedure. More services mean more revenue, regardless of whether the patient gets better. This creates a system that is structurally incentivized to treat illness rather than prevent it.

The consequences are well-documented:

- **Fragmented care**: Specialists, hospitals, and primary care physicians are each paid separately, with no payment for coordination between them.
- **Unnecessary services**: Studies estimate 15–30% of certain procedures are inappropriate or unnecessary by clinical evidence standards.
- **Misaligned incentives**: An empty hospital bed is a financial problem, even if it means the community is healthier.

## What Value-Based Care Is

[[Value-based-care|Value-based care (VBC)]] ties reimbursement to outcomes, quality, or cost efficiency rather than volume. Instead of paying per visit or procedure, payers hold providers accountable for the health of a defined patient population.

The defining feature is **risk-sharing**: if care is high-quality and costs come in below target, providers share in the savings. If costs run over or quality is poor, providers bear some of that financial consequence.

This realigns the incentives:

- Prevention becomes profitable, not just the right thing to do
- Care coordination is rewarded rather than treated as overhead
- Keeping patients out of the hospital saves money instead of costing revenue

## Why It's Needed Now

90% of U.S. healthcare spending goes toward people with chronic conditions — diabetes, heart disease, COPD. These aren't acute, one-time problems. They require continuous management, coordination, and prevention over years or decades.

Fee-for-service was designed for a different era — acute care, episodic treatment. It was never built to handle the chronic disease burden that now dominates American healthcare. VBC is the structural response to that mismatch.
