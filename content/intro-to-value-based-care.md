# Intro to Value-Based Care

**Summary**: A business model lens on VBC — why two fundamentally different healthcare systems are operating in parallel right now, and why one is winning.

**Sources**: Pearcalls, "The Healthcare Business Model That Will Define the Next Decade" (2025)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

---

## Two Systems, Same Buildings

There are currently two healthcare systems operating in the U.S. They use the same hospitals, the same doctors, the same EMRs — but run on fundamentally different rules and create completely different incentives.

One is [[fee-for-service]]: get paid per procedure, per visit, per admission. Volume drives revenue.

The other is [[value-based-care]]: get paid for keeping a population healthy. Outcomes drive revenue.

One is shrinking. The other is quietly taking over. More than half of Medicare patients are already in value-based arrangements, and CMS has set a goal for all Medicare beneficiaries to be in accountable care arrangements by 2030.

## Why Fee-for-Service Fails Chronic Care

For most of the 20th century, FFS worked reasonably well — healthcare was about fixing acute problems. You got sick, you saw a doctor, they treated you, you got better.

But **90% of the nation's $4.9 trillion in annual healthcare expenditures** (2024) now go to people with chronic conditions — diabetes, heart disease, COPD, hypertension, depression. FFS wasn't built for this world:

- **Prevention doesn't pay.** A 30-minute conversation preventing diabetes complications might bill at $150. The eventual amputation bills at $50,000. The incentive is backward.
- **Coordination isn't rewarded.** When a patient sees five specialists, nobody gets paid to make sure they're aligned. The primary care physician who spends hours coordinating care does so for free under FFS.
- **Volume trumps value.** An empty hospital bed is a financial problem even if it means the community is healthier.
- **Fragmentation is structural.** Every specialist, lab, hospital, and PCP is a separate billing entity with no shared accountability for the patient's overall health trajectory.

## How VBC Flips the Incentives

Under VBC, providers take financial responsibility for a population's health outcomes. Keep patients healthy and costs down — the provider shares in the savings. Let preventable problems become expensive crises — the provider bears some of the cost.

This changes every incentive:

| FFS Incentive | VBC Incentive |
|---|---|
| More visits = more revenue | Healthy patients = better margins |
| Hospital admission generates revenue | Preventing hospitalizations saves money |
| Specialist referral generates a billing event | Unnecessary referrals cost money |
| Prevention is overhead | Prevention is the core business |
| Coordination has no billing code | Care managers reduce costly events |

## The Mechanics

**Shared savings**: Providers continue receiving FFS payments but also receive a spending target based on historical costs. If total spending comes in below that target, they keep a percentage of the savings (typically 40–75%). This is how the Medicare Shared Savings Program ([[compare-aco-reach-mssp|MSSP]]) works, and in 2024 it generated $2.4–2.5 billion in net Medicare savings across 476 ACOs and 14.3 million beneficiaries.

**Capitation**: Providers receive a fixed amount per patient per month to cover all care. Profit comes from keeping patients healthy. This is the model used in [[medicare|Medicare Advantage]] — which now covers 34.4 million people (55% of Medicare) — and in advanced ACO models like ACO REACH.

**Quality metrics**: Cost reduction alone isn't enough. VBC contracts include extensive quality benchmarks — Star Ratings in Medicare Advantage, HEDIS measures, patient experience scores, chronic disease outcome measures — that determine whether providers share in savings and by how much. This prevents the cost reduction from happening through rationing rather than better care.

**Bundled payments**: A single payment for an entire episode of care — say, a hip replacement including surgery, hospital stay, and 90-day rehabilitation. The provider entity manages the full cycle and keeps whatever isn't spent. This is a structural approach to the coordination problem.

## Why Now

Three forces converged to make modern VBC viable at scale:

**1. Data infrastructure**: Electronic health records and claims data now allow providers to track population health, identify high-risk patients before they become expensive, and intervene proactively. Ten years ago this data wasn't available or usable. AI tools are accelerating this further — surfacing coding gaps, care gaps, and risk patterns at scale.

**2. Regulatory push**: CMS has systematically expanded ACO and Medicare Advantage programs, creating the payment architecture VBC requires. The target is 100% of Medicare beneficiaries in accountable care arrangements by 2030 — a structural endpoint that drives every major insurer and health system's strategy.

**3. Scale tipping point**: With 14.3 million beneficiaries in MSSP ACOs and 34.4 million in MA, value-based infrastructure is now large enough to be the default rather than the exception. The clinical IT vendors, analytics platforms, and care management companies built for VBC now have the volume to drive unit economics down. The upfront investment threshold is lower than it was in 2015.

## Who is Winning and Who is Struggling

High-performing VBC organizations share consistent characteristics:
- **Strong primary care foundation** — VBC is built on primary care. Organizations without engaged PCPs find population health management nearly impossible.
- **Real-time data capabilities** — Claims data lags 60–90 days. Winners use clinical data streams to intervene before events.
- **Physician-led governance** — ACOs governed by physicians tend to reduce utilization more aggressively than hospital-owned ACOs, which face FFS revenue protection conflicts.
- **Scale** — Infrastructure costs are largely fixed; larger populations spread that cost and create statistical stability.

The transition is hardest for **independent primary care practices** and **rural providers** — they face the same infrastructure investment requirements but lack the capital and data capabilities of large health systems. Many are partnering with or selling to larger networks, accelerating [[healthcare-consolidation|consolidation]].

## The 2026 State of the Transition

VBC is now operational infrastructure rather than pilot programming, but the shift is far from complete:

- **55%** of Medicare beneficiaries are in fully capitated Medicare Advantage
- **~53%** of traditional Medicare beneficiaries are in some accountable care relationship
- Commercial insurance VBC adoption lags Medicare significantly — most commercial contracts remain primarily FFS with quality overlays
- Most providers operate in **dual-track mode**: running VBC care management infrastructure while simultaneously billing FFS — a costly and operationally complex state
- CMS's ACO REACH program concludes at end of 2026; its replacement, the **LEAD Model**, launches in 2027 with a 10-year runway
