# Employer-Based Insurance

**Summary**: The dominant source of health coverage in the U.S., providing insurance to roughly 156 million Americans through their jobs or a family member's job.

## Why the U.S. Is Employer-Based

Unlike most peer nations with universal public systems, the U.S. relies on employers to voluntarily provide insurance — a path that emerged largely by historical accident. During World War II, wage controls made health benefits one of the few ways employers could compete for workers. After the war, the tax treatment was codified: employer contributions to group health insurance are not treated as taxable income for employees, making employer-provided benefits more valuable than equivalent cash wages. This tax preference remains the structural glue of the employer-based system.

The **ERISA exemption** (1974 Employee Retirement Income Security Act) further entrenched employer-based coverage by allowing self-insured employer plans to escape state insurance regulations, including mandated benefit requirements. More than half of all group insurance coverage is now provided through self-insurance.

## Scale and Cost

- ESI is the largest source of health coverage in the U.S., covering roughly 156 million Americans
- A **family of four** with employer-sponsored coverage contributed **$6,296 in premiums** and incurred **$3,564 in out-of-pocket spending** in 2023 (Source: KFF/Peterson)
- Private insurance costs are expected to rise further in 2026

## Self-Insurance

More than half of all employer-sponsored coverage is now self-insured. In self-insured plans:
- The employer bears the financial risk of health claims rather than paying premiums to an insurer
- The employer typically contracts with an insurer for administrative services only (ASO arrangement)
- Stop-loss insurance limits exposure to catastrophic individual claims
- The employer is exempt from state insurance regulations under ERISA, including mandated benefit laws

Self-insured membership is estimated to grow at ~1% annually through 2029, with stronger growth from 2027 as some Medicaid disenrollees transition into employer plans. Margins for ASO (third-party administrator) arrangements are expected to expand 20–30% between 2024 and 2029.

## The ACA's Employer Mandate

The [[affordable-care-act|ACA]] requires employers with 50+ full-time equivalent employees to offer coverage meeting minimum value (60% actuarial value) and affordability (≤9.02% of employee household income for self-only coverage, 2025) standards, or pay penalties. Employers offering coverage that fails either test give employees the option to seek subsidized Marketplace coverage instead.

## 2026 Outlook: Group Insurance as Growth Driver

The group insurance segment is expected to become the **largest contributor to payer EBITDA by 2029**, reaching $27 billion (36% share), up from $9 billion in 2024. Growth drivers include:

- **Medicaid disenrollment pipeline**: Of the ~9 million expected to lose Medicaid coverage under [[obbba|OBBBA]], about 6 million are already working part- or full-time. Insurers and employers have an opportunity to facilitate their transition to group plans.
- **Economic growth**: McKinsey estimates 1–2 million additional employer-sponsored lives from jobs created 2026–2029
- **Premium adjustments**: Revenue per member per year in large group expected to rise 2–3% annually through 2027 to offset provider reimbursement increases

However, EBITDA margins in large group are unlikely to return to historical 2–3% range due to reimbursement pressures and GLP-1 drug costs, settling below 2% by 2029.

## Historical Context

The 1992 OECD paper noted that most employers who do not offer coverage are in service industries (restaurants, retail) with younger, healthier workers, while industries with unionized older workforces (auto, steel) provided the most comprehensive benefits. This split created divergent business interests on health reform that persist today.
