# The Uninsured

**Summary**: Approximately 26–28 million Americans lacked health insurance as of 2024 — a number projected to grow significantly due to OBBBA Medicaid cuts and ACA subsidy expiration, reversing the gains made under the ACA.

**Sources**: `Eight Trends Shaping 2026 Healthcare Costs.md`, `What to expect in US healthcare in 2026 and beyond.md`, `A layman's guide to the U.S. health care system.md`, `The Affordable Care Act 101.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-12

---

## Current Scale

- **~26–28 million** Americans uninsured as of 2024
- The uninsured rate reached a record low of **7.7%** in 2023, down from 14–16% before the [[affordable-care-act|ACA]]
- CBO projects approximately **10 million additional people** will become uninsured as a result of [[obbba|OBBBA]] healthcare provisions

The projected 10 million breaks down as:
- ~7–8 million from Medicaid disenrollment (work requirements, provider tax changes, enrollment redeterminations)
- ~2 million from ACA Marketplace changes (end of auto-renewals, stricter verification, subsidy changes)

## Who the Uninsured Are

Being uninsured in the U.S. is not primarily a characteristic of people outside the workforce. The majority of the uninsured are **workers or their dependents**. Key patterns from the historical record, which remain structurally similar today:

- **75% of the uninsured are employees or their dependents** — working in jobs that don't offer insurance or where coverage is unaffordable (Source: historical OECD data, patterns persist)
- Industries with high uninsured rates: service sector (restaurants, retail, personal care), construction, agriculture
- **Income**: Most uninsured fall in a band too high for Medicaid (in non-expansion states) but too low to easily afford individual market premiums without subsidies
- **Age**: Young adults and working-age adults (19–54) have higher uninsured rates than children (covered by CHIP/Medicaid) or seniors (covered by Medicare)
- **Geography**: Uninsured rates are highest in the 10 states that have not expanded Medicaid — where ~1.4 million people fall in a "coverage gap" (too much income for Medicaid, too little for marketplace subsidies)
- **Immigration status**: Undocumented immigrants are largely ineligible for public programs; lawfully present immigrants face waiting periods

## What Happens Without Insurance

The uninsured receive fewer health services than insured individuals with comparable health status. Effects documented in research:

- Delayed and foregone care — particularly for preventive services, primary care, and chronic disease management
- Higher rates of late-stage diagnosis for conditions like cancer
- Greater reliance on emergency departments for care that could be handled in primary care settings
- Higher rates of medical debt and financial hardship

Uninsured individuals are not entirely without access to care. They can receive services through:
- **Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs)** — community health centers that provide sliding-scale care regardless of insurance status
- **Public hospitals and clinics** — particularly in urban areas
- **Emergency departments** — EMTALA requires hospitals to screen and stabilize any patient regardless of ability to pay
- **Charity care** — some hospitals and physicians provide free or reduced-fee services, financed by cross-subsidizing from paying patients

## The Cost Shift

When uninsured patients receive care without paying, the cost doesn't disappear — it is absorbed or shifted:

- **Uncompensated care** (charity care + bad debt) was ~5% of hospital expenses historically; expected to rise as uninsurance grows from OBBBA changes
- **Cost shifting**: Hospitals have historically covered uncompensated care by charging higher prices to privately insured patients and their employers, effectively making insured patients cross-subsidize the uninsured
- As [[healthcare-consolidation|market consolidation]] gives hospitals more pricing power, cost shifting becomes easier — but also makes private insurance more expensive

Rising uninsurance is one of the primary reasons provider EBITDA margins are expected to decline in 2027 before recovering. See [[hospitals]] for the financial impact.

## The ACA's Impact (and Its Limits)

The [[affordable-care-act|ACA (2010)]] reduced the uninsured rate from ~14–16% to a record low of 7.7% in 2023 through:
- Medicaid expansion (adding coverage for adults up to 138% FPL in participating states)
- ACA Marketplace subsidies (making individual market coverage affordable)
- The individual mandate (incentivizing enrollment)

The ACA did not achieve universal coverage because:
- Medicaid expansion was made optional by the 2012 Supreme Court ruling; 10 states still haven't expanded
- The individual mandate penalty was reduced to $0 in 2019
- Undocumented immigrants are ineligible
- Coverage remained unaffordable for some even with subsidies

## What Happens to OBBBA Disenrollees

McKinsey estimates that of the ~9 million expected to lose Medicaid coverage under OBBBA:
- ~1–1.5 million will transition to [[employer-based-insurance|employer-sponsored insurance]]
- ~1 million will move to [[affordable-care-act|ACA Marketplace]] coverage
- ~5.5–6.5 million will become uninsured — most are already working part- or full-time but in jobs without accessible coverage

The shift of uninsured individuals back into employer-sponsored coverage (as they take jobs with benefits or gain eligibility) is expected to drive partial provider margin recovery from 2028 onward.
